Google Will Charge You for Missed Calls Starting October 1 — Here's What to Fix This Week

For home-service contractors running Google Local Services Ads. Plain English, no marketing jargon.

If you run Google Local Services Ads — the listings with the Google Verified badge at the top of search results — something changes on October 1, 2026, that will quietly raise what you pay for leads.

Starting October 1, Google will charge you for some calls you never answered. A missed call during your business hours becomes a billable lead if the caller stays on the line more than 20 seconds — about five rings.

Until now, a missed call cost you the customer. From October 1, it can cost you the customer and the lead fee.

What exactly is changing

Three parts of the new rule matter:

  1. Missed calls can be billed. Caller waits more than 20 seconds during your business hours, nobody picks up — Google can treat it as a valid lead and charge you.
  2. Follow-up calls count too. If the first call isn't charged, a later call with the same customer can be charged instead (once per customer per 15 days). The meter just moved.
  3. It stacks on top of existing responsiveness signals. Google frames this as rewarding responsiveness — and responsiveness already influences where your ads show.

Sources: PPC Land · AdStack · Invoca · Small Business Currents

What it costs you

Do your own math: take your average LSA lead cost and multiply by the calls you miss each month.

AdStack's example: leads at $80, twenty missed calls a month — not a high number for a shop where two people answer phones between jobs. That's $1,600 a month for conversations that never happened. Plus the jobs those callers gave to whoever picked up.

And here's the part that stings: 91% of emergency jobs go to the first contractor who answers. The caller you missed didn't just cost you $80 — they booked your competitor.

Why small shops get hit hardest

Big companies have dispatchers. You have one phone and a job half-done. The calls that ring out are the ones that come in while you're on a roof, under a sink, or driving between jobs — and those are exactly the calls this change starts billing for. "Just answer every call" is easy to say from a desk. It's not a plan.

What to fix this week — 5 things

1. Audit your business hours in Local Services. The 20-second rule only applies during your stated business hours. If your hours are wrong — or wider than your real coverage — you're paying for calls you were never going to answer. Tighten them to reality.

2. Know your miss rate. Open individual phone leads in your LSA lead inbox and read the conversation log — it shows "Missed call" per lead. There's no aggregate view, so spot-check 20 leads and do the math. You can't fix a gap you can't see. (Source: AdGenius, missed-call FAQ)

3. Put a text-back on every missed call. This is the single highest-leverage fix. When a caller gets an automatic text within a minute — "Sorry we missed you, what do you need help with?" — three things happen: the caller waits instead of dialing your competitor, you get the lead in writing, and you reply when you're off the ladder. It doesn't require answering faster. It requires responding faster, automatically.

4. Learn the dispute process. Google says it has safeguards against spam and robocalls, but hasn't explained how they work. LSA lets you dispute leads from the inbox — with this charge brand new, dispute anything questionable immediately; the credit policy here is still untested.

5. Watch the follow-up rule. A caller who hangs up at 15 seconds and rings back later can still become a billable lead. Your callback list is now a billing surface too — work it fast, because every hour of delay is a job aging toward your competitor.

The bottom line

Google framed this as rewarding responsiveness. Fair enough. But the practical effect is simple: every unanswered ring now costs you twice — once on the ad bill, once in the job that walked away.

The shops that come out ahead won't be the ones with the biggest ad budgets. They'll be the ones that stopped letting a ringing phone be the end of the conversation.
